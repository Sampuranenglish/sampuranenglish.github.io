A Rising Graph: On the Doping Culture in Indian Sports

Editorial · for exam practice

At least 13 doping positives in the run-up to the recent Commonwealth Games and the upcoming Asian Games have again put India in an embarrassing spotlight. For a country that has been notoriously leading the global doping charts for three consecutive years, the spate of positive results across disciplines including athletics, weightlifting, wrestling, wushu and judo, has not only brought shame but has also robbed India of some berths in these prestigious events. The dope-tainted names have forced authorities to pull them out of the Aichi-Nagoya Asian Games squad. More such names are expected to trickle out in the coming weeks. Lucrative cash incentives for Asian Games medallists are a major factor driving athletes and coaches to take shortcuts, risking their health and reputation, as well as the country’s pride.

Subject–Verb
① At least 13 doping positives (in the run-up to the Games) — have put India in an embarrassing spotlight.
② the spate (of positive results across disciplines) — has brought shame, has robbed berths.

While the spike in dope-positive results ahead of major multi-sport events is not new, the jump this time is a cause for concern. The matter getting raised during the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) workshops in Delhi, the Athletics Integrity Unit (AIU) downgrading India due to an extremely high risk of doping, and an international agency recently conducting surprise tests in Bhubaneswar and Bengaluru indicate the seriousness of the issue. Even though National Sports Federations have been expressing concern, it cannot be said that they are strict to the core to curb the menace.

Subject–Verb
③ the spike (in dope-positive results ahead of major multi-sport events) — is not new.
④ The matter (getting raised at WADA workshops, the AIU downgrading India, and an agency’s surprise tests — three linked subjects) — indicate the seriousness of the issue.

Despite being seriously understaffed, the National Anti-Doping Agency (NADA), which shoulders the primary anti-doping responsibility in India, has significantly increased the number of tests and raids based on intelligence gathering. But it needs to do more in a vast and populous country to discourage doping. Besides, NADA needs to get out of the government’s control to function truly independently in order to enhance its effectiveness. Proper implementation of the National Anti-Doping (Amendment) Act, 2025, to give more powers to NADA and its allied bodies, is another requirement.

Subject–Verb
⑤ the National Anti-Doping Agency (NADA) (which shoulders the primary anti-doping responsibility) — has increased the number of tests and raids.
⑥ Proper implementation (of the National Anti-Doping (Amendment) Act, 2025, to give more powers to NADA and its allied bodies) — is another requirement.

With dope testing becoming costlier and NADA needing more staff, the government can bolster the fight against doping by increasing the overall budget allocation, which was reduced in the current budget. The other challenge is to cut the supply line, as India has become a hub for the trade in banned substances sourced from different countries. An Indian serving a jail term in Kenya for illegally importing performance-enhancing drugs stands as proof of this. As India prepares for the 2030 Commonwealth Games and hopes to host the Olympics in the future, it needs to act resolutely to weed out a deep-rooted doping culture in Indian sports.

The editorial credits NADA for increasing tests and raids "despite being seriously understaffed," while firmly arguing it "needs to do more" and requires independence and better funding — critical but constructive, not celebratory (A), dismissive (C), or hopeless (D), since concrete remedies are proposed throughout.

The editorial reports the opposite: the AIU downgraded India due to an "extremely high risk of doping," which is a warning sign, not an upgrade to a low-risk category. A, B, and C are all directly stated as genuine concerns or remedies in the passage.

Analysis

16 Sep 2026

Core Message

  • At least 13 doping positives ahead of the Commonwealth Games and Asian Games have again exposed India's persistent doping problem, costing the country places in prestigious events.
  • Warning signs — WADA workshops, an AIU downgrade, and surprise international testing — show the issue is worsening, even as National Sports Federations remain insufficiently strict.
  • NADA, though understaffed and under government control, has increased tests and raids, but needs independence, stronger legal powers under the 2025 Amendment Act, and a larger budget to cut both demand and the cross-border supply of banned substances.

Inference

  • The link drawn between cash incentives and doping implies that reward structures, if unaccompanied by stronger deterrents, can unintentionally encourage risky behaviour among athletes and coaches.
  • NADA's rising number of tests despite being understaffed suggests the true scale of doping in India may be larger than official figures currently reveal.
  • The mention of an Indian jailed in Kenya for importing performance-enhancing drugs implies that domestic enforcement alone cannot solve the problem without international cooperation to disrupt supply networks.

Purpose

To alert readers to the scale and persistence of doping in Indian sports ahead of major events, and to press the government and sporting bodies to grant NADA genuine independence, adequate funding, and stronger legal powers to address both the demand for and supply of banned substances.

Tone

The editorial opens with concern and mild alarm over the embarrassing spate of positive tests, moves through a measured, evidence-based account of institutional shortcomings and NADA's partial progress, and closes with a firm, forward-looking call for resolute action before the 2030 Commonwealth Games and a possible Olympic bid.
