Shifting sands: On the Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Türkiye Joint Defence Agreement

The Hindu Editorial · reproduced as published, for exam practice

The joint defence agreement, signed by Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Türkiye in Mecca on August 7 reflects their shared strategic anxieties and the changes under way in West Asia's security architecture. The three countries have pledged to bolster collective security and deterrence, and promised to treat an armed attack against one as an attack against all. On paper, this is a formidable alliance. Pakistan is a nuclear-armed country with a powerful military; Saudi Arabia, one of the world's largest crude oil exporters, has deep pockets and considerable influence across the Muslim world; and Türkiye has an expanding military industrial base, including advanced drone production capabilities.

Short form: "The joint defence agreement.... reflects (their shared strategic anxieties and the changes under way in West Asia's security architecture.)"
Subject–Verb
② Saudi Arabia ... has (deep pockets and considerable influence across the Muslim world) — separated by the appositive ", one of the world's largest crude oil exporters," describing Saudi Arabia before the verb arrives. Short form: "Saudi Arabia.... has (deep pockets and considerable influence across the Muslim world.)"

That the pact was announced in the middle of the war on Iran and Israel's continuing bombing campaigns in the region is no coincidence. Faced with the prospect of reduced American strategic presence in West Asia, regional powers have begun looking beyond the traditional U.S. security umbrella for new partnerships. In September 2025, a few weeks after Israel bombed Qatar, Pakistan and Saudi Arabia announced a bilateral defence agreement.

Short form: "That the pact was announced... ... is (no coincidence.)"

The war on Iran, which saw Tehran retaliating by striking U.S. bases across the Persian Gulf, and Israel's wars in its neighbourhood, have further deepened the security concerns of Saudi Arabia and Türkiye. They have now decided to turn the page on their often fraught relationship and build a trilateral framework with Pakistan, which fought a short aerial war with India in May 2025.

④ The war on Iran, ... and Israel's wars in its neighbourhood ... have further deepened (the security concerns of Saudi Arabia and Türkiye) — separated by the relative clause ", which saw Tehran retaliating by striking U.S. bases across the Persian Gulf," inserted between the two halves of the compound subject and the verb. Short form: "The war on Iran, and Israel's wars in its neighbourhood, have further deepened (the security concerns of Saudi Arabia and Türkiye.)"

This agreement shows the growing appetite of regional powers for formal security pacts. Türkiye, which has long sought a bigger role in West Asia, will gain more strategic space. Pakistan, traditionally a South Asian regional power overshadowed by India, is also seeking to expand its role as a West Asia security provider and mediator. The alliance would allow the three countries to deepen defence cooperation and military interoperability though it is too early to say whether it would help them address their security challenges. Alliances based on collective security, such as NATO and CSTO, are generally formed to build deterrence against conflicts.

Analysis

Core Message

  • The editorial examines the trilateral defence agreement signed by Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and Türkiye in Mecca on August 7, framing it as a response to shared anxieties over West Asia's shifting security landscape.
  • It highlights each country's distinct strategic assets — Pakistan's nuclear capability, Saudi Arabia's financial and religious influence, and Türkiye's growing military-industrial base, particularly in drones — as the foundation of this "formidable" alliance on paper.
  • The piece situates the pact within a broader pattern: declining confidence in the U.S. security umbrella, the September 2025 Pakistan-Saudi bilateral pact, and the deepening anxieties triggered by the war on Iran and Israel's regional bombing campaigns.
  • It closes by noting that while the agreement reflects growing appetite for formal security pacts, much like NATO or CSTO, it remains too early to say whether it will genuinely address the three countries' underlying security challenges.

Inference

  • Describing the timing of the pact as "no coincidence" alongside the war on Iran and Israel's bombing campaigns implies the author sees the agreement as a deliberate response to regional instability rather than routine diplomacy.
  • Noting that Saudi Arabia and Türkiye have "decided to turn the page on their often fraught relationship" implies their past differences were serious enough to require an explicit, deliberate reconciliation.
  • Highlighting Pakistan's ambition to become "a West Asia security provider and mediator" despite being "traditionally a South Asian regional power" implies the author views Pakistan's expanding role as a notable departure from its historical position.
  • Ending on the phrase "too early to say" implies the author is skeptical that a formal pact alone guarantees the practical cooperation and results these countries are seeking.

Purpose

To analyse the trilateral Pakistan-Saudi Arabia-Türkiye defence pact as a response to regional anxieties over reduced U.S. strategic presence, while cautioning that the agreement's symbolic strength does not yet guarantee it will resolve the underlying security challenges facing its signatories.

Tone

The passage opens descriptive and analytical while outlining the pact's terms and each country's strategic assets, turns explanatory as it traces the regional events that led to the agreement, and closes on a measured, cautious note that withholds judgement on the alliance's eventual effectiveness.
